Gantry Cranes

Gantry Cranes for Yards and Buildings That Cannot Carry a Runway

Full and semi-gantry cranes run on floor rails instead of elevated runways — heavy lifting for laydown areas, yards and structures that were never designed to take a crane load.

The configuration decision

Full Gantry or Semi-Gantry: How Much Structure Do You Already Have?

Both put the crane load into the floor instead of the building. The question is whether the crane stands on two legs — or borrows one side from a structure you already own.

Full Gantry

A complete portal frame — two legs on floor rails, in single or double girder form. The building carries nothing.

Choose it when

  • The work is outdoors — a yard, a laydown area, a storage strip
  • The building structure cannot take runway loads, and reinforcing it costs more than rails
  • Single girder for lighter duties, double girder where capacity and span demand it

Semi-Gantry

One leg on a floor rail, the other end running on an existing runway or wall — single or double girder.

Choose it when

  • One side of the bay can carry rail — the other cannot
  • You are extending coverage alongside an existing overhead crane
  • Floor space on one side must stay clear of legs and rails

The rail and its foundation are part of the specification — which is one more reason the free site visit pays for itself before anything is ordered.

Sizing it properly

Four Things Decide the Right Gantry

  • 01.

    Span

    Rail to rail across the working strip — and how much of it the load actually crosses.

  • 02.

    Capacity

    The heaviest lift, not the average one — rated for the worst day in the yard.

  • 03.

    Travel length

    How far along the rails the crane works — it sets the rail run, the power feed and the cost of the civil work.

  • 04.

    Ground and rail

    The foundation under the rail carries everything. Outdoors, it is half the engineering.
